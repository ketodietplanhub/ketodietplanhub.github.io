Keto for Women

Women's bodies respond to keto in ways that differ from men's, and the standard advice does not always fit. Hormones, menstrual cycles, and a tendency to lose weight more slowly all shape the experience. This guide explains what women should know about keto, the issues that commonly come up, and how to adapt the diet so it works for you.

Hormones and the menstrual cycle

Many women notice changes to their cycle when they start keto. Periods may become irregular for a few months, arrive earlier or later than usual, or feel different. This is usually because the body is adjusting to a new fuel source and to weight loss, both of which can affect hormone levels. For some women with polycystic ovary syndrome, keto can improve cycles and insulin sensitivity over time. If your cycle changes dramatically, or stops for more than three months, talk to your doctor rather than assuming it is normal.

Weight loss differences

Women often lose weight more slowly than men on keto, and this is normal. Women typically have less muscle mass, which means a lower metabolic rate, and hormonal fluctuations across the month can cause water retention that masks fat loss on the scales. Comparing your progress with a partner or a friend is discouraging and unhelpful. Track your measurements and how your clothes fit as well as your weight, and judge progress over months, not days. A steady loss of half a kilo a week is a good result.

Calories, thyroid, and energy

Very low calorie intake combined with keto can be stressful for the body, and some women find that eating too little leads to fatigue, hair shedding, or hormonal disruption. If you feel cold, tired, and sluggish, you may be under-eating. Make sure you eat enough fat and do not drop calories too aggressively. Thyroid function can also be affected by rapid weight loss, so if you have a thyroid condition, monitor it with your doctor when you start keto. Prioritise sleep, manage stress, and avoid combining keto with intense fasting until you are well adapted.

Keto, pregnancy, and menopause

Keto is not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding without medical supervision, because the developing baby needs a reliable supply of glucose and nutrients. If you are planning a pregnancy, discuss your diet with your doctor. During perimenopause and menopause, many women find keto helps with weight gain around the middle and with blood sugar control, but hot flushes and sleep can still be challenging. Focus on nutrient-dense foods, calcium-rich foods like cheese and leafy greens, and adequate protein to protect muscle and bone as you age.

Tracking progress beyond the scales

The bathroom scales only tell part of the story, and for women they can be especially misleading, because water retention changes through the month. Take measurements once a month — waist, hips, and thighs — and take a photo in the same clothes and lighting each month, so you can see changes the scales miss. Notice how your clothes fit, how your energy feels, and how you sleep. Keep a simple note of your cycle, your weight, and your energy each week, and patterns will emerge that help you understand your body. If the scales have not moved for a month but your measurements have, you are still making progress, and the scales are simply the wrong tool for that week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my period irregular on keto?

Hormonal shifts from changing your diet and losing weight can disturb cycles for a few months. If irregularity lasts more than three months or periods stop, see your doctor.

Should women eat more carbs on keto?

Some women do better with a slightly higher carb intake, around 50 grams of net carbs, especially if they are very active or struggling with energy. It is fine to adjust to what works for you.

Can keto help with PCOS?

Some studies suggest keto can improve insulin sensitivity and hormone balance in women with PCOS, but it is not a cure, and you should work with your doctor on a plan.
