Keto Macros Explained

Macros — short for macronutrients — are the three nutrients that provide energy: fat, protein, and carbohydrate. On keto, the ratio between them is what makes the diet work. This guide explains what each macro does, how to calculate your own targets, and how to track them in real life without becoming obsessed.

Fat, protein, and carbs on keto

Fat is your main fuel on keto, providing 70 to 75 percent of your calories. It comes from butter, olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty meat, and cheese. Protein, at 20 to 25 percent of calories, builds and repairs muscle and keeps you full; good sources are meat, fish, eggs, and dairy. Carbohydrates are kept to 5 to 10 percent, usually 20 to 50 grams of net carbs a day, which is low enough to keep you in ketosis. Fat has 9 calories per gram, while protein and carbohydrate each have 4, which is why a small amount of fat carries a lot of energy.

Calculating your macros

Start with your daily calorie target, which depends on your weight, height, age, sex, and activity level. Then apply the keto split: for 2,000 calories a day, 70 percent fat is about 156 grams, 25 percent protein is about 125 grams, and 5 percent carbs is about 25 grams. Use the macro calculator on our home page to get your own numbers. Protein is the macro to hit most precisely — too little and you lose muscle, far too much and it can make ketosis harder. Fat is flexible: eat enough to feel full, but remember that extra fat is extra calories if weight loss is your goal.

Net carbs and tracking

On keto, you count net carbs — total carbs minus fibre — because fibre is not digested into glucose. A food with 10 grams of total carbs and 6 grams of fibre has 4 grams of net carbs. In some countries, labels already show net carbs, so check how your local labels work. Track your food for the first two to four weeks using an app or a notebook, weighing portions when you can. Most people are surprised by how quickly carbs add up, and tracking is the fastest way to learn your foods. After a few weeks, many people can estimate accurately without tracking every meal.

Adjusting your macros

Your macros are not fixed for life. As you lose weight, your calorie needs fall, so recalculate every few months. If you start exercising more, you may need more protein and calories. If weight loss stalls for several weeks, check whether portions have crept up or whether you are eating too many keto treats. Some people do well with a slightly higher carb limit of 50 grams, while others need to stay under 20 grams to maintain ketosis. Pay attention to your energy, hunger, and results, and adjust one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

A worked example day

Here is what a day at 2,000 calories and a 70-25-5 split looks like in real food. Breakfast is two eggs fried in butter with spinach and half an avocado, giving about 25 grams of fat, 15 grams of protein, and 3 grams of net carbs. Lunch is a large chicken salad with olive oil dressing, cheese, and nuts, giving about 40 grams of fat, 35 grams of protein, and 5 grams of net carbs. Dinner is salmon with buttered broccoli and cauliflower mash, giving about 45 grams of fat, 35 grams of protein, and 7 grams of net carbs. A snack of Greek yoghurt with berries adds the rest. The totals come to roughly 155 grams of fat, 125 grams of protein, and 25 grams of net carbs, which is close to the target, and the meals are normal, satisfying food.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to track macros forever?

No. Most people track for a few weeks to learn their foods, then track again only when progress stalls or their weight changes significantly.

What is the difference between total carbs and net carbs?

Net carbs are total carbs minus fibre. Fibre is not digested into glucose, so it does not count against your keto carb limit.

Can I eat too much protein on keto?

It is possible, but harder than people think. Very high protein intake can make ketosis harder, but moderate amounts — around 1.2 to 2 grams per kilogram of body weight — are fine for most people.
